The Long Memory of Tides

By A. Mariner

ShareTweetEmail

Twice a day, on nearly every shore on Earth, the sea remembers the moon. The water rises without hurry, holds for a moment as if listening, and withdraws again along the same wet path it came by. People have set their clocks, their harvests, and their wars by this rhythm for as long as there have been people near the water to watch it happen.

The mechanism is old news — Newton had the essentials by 1687 — but the consequences still surprise. The tide is not a wave that travels so much as a bulge the planet rotates beneath, and the friction of that endless dragging is slowing the Earth itself. Days are getting longer by about two milliseconds a century, paid for out of the moon's orbit.

What the estuaries know

In an estuary the arithmetic becomes negotiation. River water stacks against the incoming sea, and the tide arrives not as a line on a chart but as a personality: early here, late there, doubled in one channel and cancelled in the next. Pilots on the Severn or the Qiantang will tell you the schedule is only advice.

The sea refuses no river, the old line goes — but it names its price at every mouth.

The extreme case is the tidal bore, where the leading edge of the flood steepens into a single travelling wall of water. The Qiantang bore can stand nine metres tall and moves fast enough that spectators are warned off the banks hours in advance.

Counting on it

Because tides are astronomical at heart, they are predictable in a way almost nothing else in nature is. The standard method reduces a station's tide to a sum of harmonic constituents:

h(t) = Z0 + Σ Ai · cos(ωi · t + φi)   // per constituent i

Nineteenth-century engineers built brass machines — Kelvin's tide predictors — that summed those cosines with pulleys and wire, printing a year of tables in a day. The same arithmetic, in software, still anchors every published tide table.

Living by the clock of it

Whole ecologies are timed to the cycle. Intertidal creatures carry circatidal clocks that keep ticking in a lab aquarium far from any coast, opening and closing on schedule for the sea they can no longer feel. Some shorebirds feed by the tide and rest by the sun, running both clocks at once.

  • Spring tides follow the full and new moon, when sun and moon pull together.
  • Neap tides follow the quarters, when they pull at right angles.
  • The highest springs of all arrive when the moon is also at perigee.

None of this is mysterious anymore, and all of it is still worth standing on a harbour wall for. The tide is the rare natural force that is both entirely explained and entirely undiminished by the explanation — a clockwork that reads as breathing.
